### Christian or Northern Renaissance Humanism
- Christian Humanists cultivated a knowledge of classics, like their Italian counterparts
- They focused on the sources of early Christianity, the bible, and writings of church fathers such as Augustine, Ambrose, and Jerome.
	- They found a simple religion that they believed had been distorted by the complex theological arguments of the middle ages 
- Believed that through studying classical antiquity and educating people about it, they could instill an inner piety that would bring about reform in the church.
	- As such, they supported schools, and the ability for everyone to be able to read the bible in their native language.
	- [[Erasmus]], in his preface to his Greek translation of the New Testament, argued that women should be able to read as well
- Believed very deeply in the power of education, some called them naive, but they were simply optimistic
- Major Christian Humanists include [[Erasmus]] and [[Thomas More]]
- Erasmus:
	- Emphasized Christianity as a "philosophy of Christ"
	- Translated the New Testament and wrote *[[The Praise of Folly]]*.
- Thomas More:
	- Wrote *[[Utopia]]* in 1516
	- Argued for what is basically communism, but came face-to-face with corruption when he served Henry VIII
		- Was persecuted by the English for following the Catholic Church
### Church and Religion on the Eve of the Reformation
- Corruption in the Church made people want to reform
- Failure of Renaissance popes to provide spiritual leadership affected all of Christendom
- Focus on money led to nobles and the wealthy holding positions of power in the Church
	- To increase revenue, they would hold multiple offices in the Church, a practice known as **pluralism**.
	- Because they could only attend one office at a time, this lead to absenteeism, where officials hired other people, often unqualified, to do their work.
- Complaints about the ignorance and ineptness of priests became widespread in the 15th century
    #### The Search for Salvation
	 - With leaders failing to provide guidance, people struggled to find salvation
	 - For some, this process became almost mechanical
		 - Frederick the Wise, Martin Luther's prince, amassed 19,000 relics to which were attached indulgences which could reduce one's time in purgatory by nearly 2 million years.
	- Others sought certainty of salvation in a mystical movement known as the Modern Devotion, which downplayed dogma, stressed the need to follow the teachings of Christ.
		- Thomas a Kempis wrote *The Imitation of Christ* and argued that at the end of the day people will be judged solely on how religiously they lived.
	- Most of these movements stayed within the framework of the Catholic Church, but disillusionment with the Church would grow as the church did not keep up with their expectations.
  #### Calls for Reform
	- Many sources of reform were already at work within the church in the late 15th and early 16th centuries
	- Notably, calls for reform came from Franciscan, Dominican, and Augustinian orders.
		- These orders placed an emphasis on preaching to laypeople
	- Johannes Geiler of Kaiserberg was a popular preacher who denounced the corruption of clergy.
	- The Oratory of Divine Love, founded in 1497, was not a religious order, but a group that worked to foster reform in the Church.
	- Emphasized personal spiritual development and outward acts of charity.
	- The philosophy of Christ (see [[Erasmus]]) was appealing to many of them.
	- Members included cardinals who favored church reform.
		- Cardinal Ximenes was especially active in using Christian Humanism to reform the Church, he had many spiritual writings, including *The Imitation of Christ*, translated into Spanish.
